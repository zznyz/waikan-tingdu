For centuries the ancient game of Go was seen as a stronghold of human intelligence that no computer could conquer. Chess had already fallen to machines, but Go was different: the number of possible positions is greater than the atoms in the universe, so pure calculation was not enough. Winning seemed to require something like feeling — a sense of shape and balance that masters spend a lifetime developing. That belief was shaken in 2016, when a program called AlphaGo faced Lee Sedol, one of the greatest Go champions冠军 of his time. Few experts expected a machine to defeat击败 such a player so soon. Yet game after game, it answered its human opponent对手 with a strategy策略 that was calm, patient and strangely original. Then AlphaGo came up with a move that no one could predict预测. It made the commentators fall silent, for it broke rules that professionals had followed for hundreds of years; at first it looked like a mistake. But as the game went on, the move turned out to be brilliant, quietly deciding the result. Here was a machine showing a kind of creativity创造力 that human imagination想象力 had never reached. And yet the story did not end there. In a later game the human master replied with a move that the machine failed to see. The match was less a defeat of humanity than a fresh challenge挑战 to it: not to race machines at calculation, but to keep asking what only people can imagine, and why.
