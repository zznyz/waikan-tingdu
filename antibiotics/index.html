How Superbugs Outsmart Our Drugs
0:001:35
▶ 念到哪句，哪句高亮
英 文 原 文 · 听 读
In his Nobel lecture of 1945, Alexander Fleming, who had discovered penicillin, added a warning that few people noticed. If the drug were used carelessly, he said, bacteria could learn to survive存活 it, and whoever underdosed himself would share the blame for the next death from a resistant infection感染. He was describing evolution. Bacteria reproduce enormously fast, and every generation throws up small genetic differences. When an antibiotic is used, the few bacteria that happen to carry a defence are the ones left alive; they multiply, and their resistance耐药性 becomes ordinary. Worse, bacteria can pass such genes sideways to unrelated species, so a trait that can evolve进化 in one bacterium can spread传播 to another. Every careless use hurries the process along. Antibiotics are still handed out for colds, which are caused by viruses and cannot be touched by them. Courses are abandoned the moment a patient feels better. In many countries the drugs are given to healthy farm animals as a routine常规的 measure. The cost is already measurable. One large international study estimated that in 2019 about 1.27 million deaths worldwide were caused directly by bacteria that no longer answered to the drugs meant to kill them — more than malaria. Operations we think of as safe depend on antibiotics working: without reliable treatment治疗, a hip replacement becomes a serious gamble. None of this is a distant threat威胁. Fleming saw it in 1945, in the very speech in which he was handed a prize for the cure.
