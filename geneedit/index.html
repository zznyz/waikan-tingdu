A Cure Built for One

英 文 原 文 · 听 读
In February 2025, doctors in Philadelphia made medical history. For the first time ever, a gene-editing therapy was designed to treat just one single patient. That patient was a baby boy named KJ, born with a rare罕见的 genetic disease that his body could not fight on its own. His liver lacked a working enzyme, so a dangerous poison slowly built up in his blood. Without help, such a condition can turn fatal within days, and ordinary treatment治疗 could only slow the damage, not cure it. So his medical team tried something no one had ever done. Instead of a drug made for millions, they built one for a single child. Using a tool called base editing, scientists designed a therapy to correct纠正 the exact spelling mistake in KJ's DNA. Tiny fatty bubbles were used to deliver输送 the editor straight to his liver cells, where the precise精确的 change could be made without cutting the DNA in two. The whole therapy—from idea to a medicine that regulators would approve批准—took only about six months. After his infusions, KJ began to eat, grow, and play, and such improvement改善 gave his family real hope. Yet the story also raises hard ethical伦理的 questions. A cure built for one child is astonishing, but who can afford it? Can such treatments ever be scaled to help thousands? Technology can be a force for good—but only if we ask, honestly, who gets to be saved.
