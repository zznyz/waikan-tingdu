Today, the Sahara is the largest hot desert on Earth, a vast广阔的 sea of sand where almost nothing grows. Yet thousands of years ago, this same land looked completely different. Rivers flowed across it, lakes filled its lowlands, and grass covered ground that is now bare rock and dune. How do we know this? The answer lies in the evidence证据 hidden beneath the sand and painted on the walls of caves. Scientists have found the dried beds of ancient古代的 lakes, along with the bones of fish, hippos and crocodiles — creatures that could never survive in today's dry heat. On rock surfaces across the region, early peoples left images of cattle, giraffes and hunters, scenes that reveal揭示 a green and living world. What changed? Researchers believe that slow shifts in the Earth's orbit altered the region's climate气候. For a long period, stronger seasonal rains turned the Sahara green. Then, as the rains weakened, the land gradually逐渐地 dried out. Grasslands gave way to sand, and the lakes disappeared. As their homeland grew harsh, the people who lived there had to migrate迁徙, moving toward rivers and valleys where water could still be found. Some evidence suggests表明 that this movement helped shape the early civilizations that later rose along great rivers. The Sahara's buried past reminds us that even the emptiest landscape may once have been full of life.
